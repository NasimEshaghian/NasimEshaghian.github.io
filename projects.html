FEATURED CASE STUDY

AI-Assisted Drinking-Water Treatment Decision Support

An applied industrial ML project exploring how historical plant data can support operational awareness and decision-making across different stages of drinking-water treatment.

Project Overview

The project focuses on developing a decision-support framework for small and rural drinking-water treatment plants, where operators may have limited time and resources for analysing process data.

The work uses real operational data from a Split Lake, Canada case study. Split Lake is a data case study for model development and evaluation, not a claim that the system is permanently deployed at that site. The framework is intended to be adaptable to different treatment plants through site-specific configuration.

The goal is not to replace operators or automate treatment decisions. It is to provide forecasts, evidence-based suggestions, and transparent assessments that operators can review before making decisions.

Industrial Machine Learning Water Treatment Operator Decision Support Applied Research

Treatment Process Context

The project considers the main treatment sequence and the relationship between water-quality measurements and chemical-treatment decisions.

Raw Water → Coagulation → Sedimentation → Filtration → Filtered Water → Chlorination → Storage Tank → Free Chlorine Residual

Turbidity data are organised using the measurement sequence Raw Water Turbidity, Filtered Water Turbidity, and Treated Water Turbidity. Chlorine residual assessment focuses on the storage tank measurement.

Three Development Phases

PHASE 01

Turbidity Early Warning

Detecting potential raw-water turbidity spikes and estimating their magnitude to provide earlier operational awareness.

PHASE 02

Coagulant Decision Support

Evaluating candidate coagulant-flow scenarios and estimating the likelihood of achieving the filtered-water turbidity objective.

PHASE 03

Chlorine Demand & Residual

Predicting the operational chlorine-demand measure and assessing free chlorine residual while retaining conservative baseline behaviour.

Phase 1 — Turbidity Early Warning & Forecasting

The first phase focused on identifying significant changes in raw-water turbidity and estimating the magnitude of potential spikes.

The modelling workflow included data auditing, timestamp alignment, feature preparation, event definition, classification, regression, and comparison with persistence baselines.

  • Audited irregular sensor readings and prepared aligned observations for chronological modelling.
  • Defined a turbidity spike as a rise of more than 2 NTU from the current observation to the next.
  • Developed a classifier using recent raw-water turbidity features to identify potential spike events.
  • Evaluated separate magnitude-estimation approaches for spike and non-spike conditions.
  • Compared candidate models against persistence rather than assuming that a more complex model was automatically better.
70% Spike-classifier precision on a 38-observation validation set
63.6% Spike-classifier recall on the same validation set
4.26 MAE for a separate spike-magnitude experiment, compared with 6.76 for persistence
0.913 vs 0.868 Non-spike Huber model MAE versus persistence; persistence remained better in this comparison

Evaluation principle: classification and magnitude estimation were treated as separate tasks. The results were reported by task and compared against a simple baseline, including cases where persistence performed better.

Phase 2 — Coagulant Decision Support

The second phase explored how historical process measurements could support operator review of coagulant-flow scenarios.

Rather than building a minimum-dose optimiser, the approach focused on estimating the likelihood that a candidate operating scenario would achieve the filtered-water turbidity objective.

  • Used a filtered-water turbidity objective below 1.00 NTU, with a 0.30 NTU tolerance and an upper acceptance boundary of 1.30 NTU.
  • Generated candidate coagulant scenarios for evaluation rather than issuing automatic dosing commands.
  • Combined model-based success probability with Bayesian-smoothed historical success probability, using a conservative estimate to avoid overstating the reliability of candidate scenarios.
  • Applied conservative probability handling and historical-support checks.
  • Returned operator-review or abstention outcomes when evidence was insufficient or a scenario was outside the supported historical range.

A key modelling constraint was the limited variation in historical dosing data. ClearPAC operation was nearly constant in the available history, while CL2410 dosing patterns were influenced by intake flow and operating state. Therefore, the project does not claim that historical associations establish a causal relationship between a particular dose and a particular turbidity outcome.

Data-leakage and causality safeguards: filtered-water turbidity was treated as an observed outcome for evaluation, not as an input feature for predicting that same outcome. No unsupported hydraulic-retention-time assumption was used.

Phase 3 — Chlorine Demand & Free Residual Assessment

The third phase addressed two related but distinct prediction tasks: the operational chlorine-demand measure and free chlorine residual.

The demand target follows the treatment system's software-calculated operational metric. It is not presented as a laboratory measurement of pre-chlorination chlorine demand. Chlorine dose was deliberately excluded from the demand model's input features.

The software-derived demand calculation uses the previous day's total chlorine, the average chlorine-dose indication over the interval, and the current day's total chlorine. The dose indication is not used as an input feature for the demand prediction model.

Free residual assessment focused on the storage-tank measurement. The current case-study configuration uses an engineer-provided target range of 0.6–2.0 mg/L. This is a configurable plant setting, not a universal target for every treatment site.

  • Prepared historical and manual observations for both demand and residual modelling.
  • Used chronological evaluation partitions to reflect the order in which operational data become available.
  • Compared candidate models against persistence baselines.
  • Retained persistence where candidate performance did not demonstrate reliable generalisation.
  • Kept candidate models in shadow evaluation rather than authorising automatic dosing.
0.40 Chlorine-demand validation MAE (53 observations)
1.33 Chlorine-demand test MAE (54 observations)
0.221 Free-residual persistence baseline MAE
0.2007 Best tested free-residual candidate MAE (Extra Trees)

Model approval outcome: the chlorine-demand candidate did not generalise sufficiently on the test data. Free-residual candidates showed only a modest improvement over persistence. The evaluated bundle therefore retained the persistence baseline, with candidate models remaining in shadow mode.

System Architecture & Operator Governance

Beyond individual models, the project considered how model outputs should be presented and governed in an operational decision-support application.

  • Canonical data layer: a consistent layer for organising measurements and observations used across the three phases.
  • Model registry: separation of approved, shadow, challenger, and experimental models.
  • Configurable site settings: plant-specific mappings, thresholds, dose grids, concentrations, and paths are kept outside hard-coded model logic.
  • Measured versus predicted data: measured API values remain the default operational state; predictions used for planning are identified separately as predictions.
  • Operator approval: recommendations remain advisory and require operator review. The system does not directly control pumps, dosing equipment, or PLCs.
  • Deployment considerations: the architecture is designed to be adaptable to different sites, including local or offline environments where required.

Decision-Support Dashboard

A Streamlit application was developed to organise the three project phases and present their outputs as separate operational decision-support workflows. The dashboard and model packaging work included configuration, model registry integration, launch scripts, and checks of application startup and model inference.

Additional evaluation and implementation notes

Phase 1 data audit: one audited extract contained 470 raw rows, of which 46 were invalid and 424 were valid. The modelling dataset contained 395 aligned pairs.

Phase 1 classifier: the 38-observation validation confusion matrix contained 24 true negatives, 3 false positives, 4 false negatives, and 7 true positives.

Phase 2 model configuration: one evaluated Random Forest configuration used 281 rows, 700 trees, maximum depth 4, minimum leaf size 5, balanced subsampling, and a probability threshold of 0.80. Its input features covered raw-water conditions, intake flow, and coagulant-flow measurements.

Phase 3 evaluation: the demand test set contained 54 records. Nine test records did not have a persistence baseline comparison. For free residual, the evaluated MAE values were 0.221 for persistence, 0.2013 for Kernel Ridge, and 0.2007 for Extra Trees.

These figures describe specific historical evaluation datasets and model experiments. They should not be interpreted as guaranteed performance at another plant or under different operating conditions.

Phase 1 classifier details: the classifier used four recent raw-water features. At the selected threshold of 0.7244, its 38-observation validation matrix contained 24 true negatives, 3 false positives, 4 false negatives, and 7 true positives.

Phase 3 data preparation: the historical SQL data were deduplicated before modelling. Manual review identified 509 usable demand days and 503 usable residual days. The evaluated deployment subsets contained 353 demand records and 358 residual records.

Configuration: plant-specific settings, including residual targets, chemical concentrations, sensor mappings, and operating thresholds, are configurable rather than fixed in the model code.

Model governance: candidate models do not automatically replace approved models or retrain themselves in production. They require evaluation and review before approval.
