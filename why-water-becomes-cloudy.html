Why Does Water Become Cloudy?

The science of suspended particles, scattered light, and why cloudy water is an engineering puzzle.

Learning Journal 5–6 min read English + فارسی

If you scoop up a glass of water from a calm stream, it may look almost transparent. Scoop up another glass after a heavy rain, and it might look more like weak tea or very runny chocolate milk.

Most of us would simply call that water cloudy or muddy. But when I started reading about drinking-water treatment, I kept running into another word: turbidity.

So what exactly is turbidity? Why does water become cloudy, how do treatment plants measure it, and why does this one number matter so much?

Light, Mud, and the Science of Scattering

Imagine driving at night. On a clear road, your headlights make the road ahead visible. But when you enter thick fog, the light starts spreading in different directions and the view becomes hazy.

Something similar happens when light passes through water containing suspended particles. Tiny pieces of clay, silt, organic material, algae, and other particles can scatter the light. That scattering makes the water look cloudy.

Rain can wash soil and other material from the surrounding land into streams, rivers, and lakes. Some particles are large enough to settle fairly quickly. Others are much smaller, and their behaviour depends on their size, surface properties, the chemistry of the water, and how much the water is moving.

That is one reason cloudy water does not always become clear just because we leave it in a bucket for a few minutes. Fine particles can stay suspended much longer than heavier grains of sand.

How Do We Measure Turbidity?

Here is an important detail: turbidity is not a direct measurement of how much mud is in the water by weight. It is an optical measurement related to how particles affect light.

Many common nephelometric turbidity meters shine light into a water sample and measure light scattered by the particles, often at an angle of 90 degrees to the incoming beam. The instrument converts that optical response into a reading reported in Nephelometric Turbidity Units (NTU).

Think of NTU as an indicator of how strongly the sample affects the light measurement—not as a weighing scale for dirt. Two water samples can have similar turbidity readings but contain different types, sizes, shapes, or colours of particles.

A turbidity number tells us something useful about the optical clarity of water, but it does not tell the whole story about what is suspended in it.

Why Does Cloudiness Matter for Drinking Water?

It is easy to think turbidity is only about appearance. Nobody wants a glass of brown water. But in a treatment plant, it matters for more practical reasons too.

1. It can make disinfection less reliable

Suspended particles can interfere with disinfection. In some conditions, microorganisms may be shielded by particles, making it harder for disinfectants such as chlorine or ultraviolet (UV) light to reach them. This is one reason effective particle removal is an important part of a multi-step drinking-water treatment process.

2. Turbidity and chlorine demand are related—but they are not the same thing

Some raw waters contain natural organic matter from soil, leaves, and other material. That organic matter can react with chlorine and consume part of it. Certain reactions between chlorine and natural organic matter can also form disinfection by-products.

But a turbidity reading does not tell us exactly how much organic matter is present. Not every particle consumes chlorine, and not every cloudy sample has the same chemical composition. That is why operators need more than one measurement to understand raw-water conditions.

3. It affects filtration

In conventional treatment, filters remove particles that remain after earlier treatment steps. If too much suspended material reaches a filter, it can load up more quickly and may need backwashing sooner. In other words, a sudden change in raw-water quality can affect how smoothly the rest of the treatment process operates.

A Practical Example: Two Glasses of Water

Picture two glasses on your kitchen counter.

  • Glass A: You stir in a little coarse sand. After a short while, many of the heavier grains settle to the bottom.
  • Glass B: You stir in very fine clay. The water becomes cloudy, and it may stay that way much longer.

The difference is not simply that one glass has more dirt. Particle size and surface behaviour matter. Fine particles can settle slowly, especially when they remain well dispersed in the water.

Water-treatment plants cannot rely on waiting for every tiny particle to settle by itself. They use treatment steps such as coagulation, flocculation, sedimentation, and filtration to remove suspended material more effectively. That is what we will explore in the next post.

What I Learned

Coming from software and machine learning, I used to imagine water quality as a row in a dataset: turbidity = 12.4.

Reading about water treatment made me look at that number differently. Turbidity is an optical signal coming from a physical mixture. The same reading can be associated with different kinds of particles and different water conditions.

When we give an AI model one turbidity value, we are giving it a useful clue—but not the complete picture. The model does not automatically know whether the particles came from fine mineral sediment, organic material, or something else. Understanding what a measurement represents, and what it leaves out, is part of building models that make sense in the real world.

The Main Takeaway

Turbidity describes the way suspended material affects light in water. It is an important indicator for drinking-water treatment, but it is not a direct measurement of particle mass, microorganism count, or chemical composition. To understand water properly, we need to look beyond one number.

تا حالا شده بعد از یه بارون حسابی به رودخونه یا سد نگاه کنی و ببینی آبش شبیه شیرکاکائو شده؟ ما معمولاً می‌گیم «آب گل‌آلود شده». اما توی مهندسی آب، برای این کدری یه اسم دقیق‌تر داریم: کدورت یا Turbidity.

شاید بپرسی خب مگه کدورت همون گل‌ولای نیست؟ نه دقیقاً. تصور کن شب داری رانندگی می‌کنی و نور چراغ ماشینت توی هوای صاف مستقیم جاده رو روشن می‌کنه. حالا وارد مه غلیظ شو؛ نور پخش می‌شه و دیدت کم می‌شه. توی آب هم ذرات ریز معلق می‌تونن نور رو پخش کنن و باعث بشن آب کدر به نظر بیاد.

وقتی بارون میاد، ممکنه ذراتی از خاک، رس، سیلت، برگ‌های پوسیده و مواد دیگه وارد رودخونه یا دریاچه بشن. بعضی ذرات درشت‌تر زود ته‌نشین می‌شن، ولی ذرات خیلی ریز می‌تونن مدت بیشتری توی آب معلق بمونن. برای همین همیشه نمی‌شه یه سطل آب کدر رو گذاشت گوشه‌ی حیاط و چند دقیقه بعد انتظار داشت مثل آب معدنی شفاف بشه!

حالا تصفیه‌خونه چطوری کدورت رو اندازه می‌گیره؟ با دستگاهی که به نمونه‌ی آب نور می‌تابونه و مقدار نوری رو که ذرات پخش می‌کنن اندازه می‌گیره. نتیجه معمولاً با واحد NTU گزارش می‌شه. نکته‌ی جالبش اینه که NTU وزن گل‌ولای نیست؛ یه اندازه‌گیری نوریه. یعنی دو نمونه آب ممکنه عدد کدورت مشابهی داشته باشن، ولی جنس ذرات داخلشون کاملاً فرق کنه.

این عدد چرا برای تصفیه‌خونه مهمه؟ چند تا دلیل داره:

  1. ضدعفونی سخت‌تر می‌شه: بعضی ذرات معلق می‌تونن از میکروارگانیسم‌ها محافظت کنن و کار کلر یا UV رو سخت‌تر کنن.
  2. کدورت با مصرف کلر یکی نیست: مواد آلی موجود در آب ممکنه با کلر واکنش بدن؛ اما عدد کدورت به‌تنهایی نمی‌گه دقیقاً چه مقدار ماده آلی توی آب داریم.
  3. فیلترها زودتر بار می‌گیرن: اگه ذرات زیادی وارد فیلتر بشن، فیلتر ممکنه زودتر نیاز به شست‌وشوی معکوس پیدا کنه.

برای من جالب‌ترین بخش ماجرا این بود که فهمیدم عددی که توی دیتاست فقط یه ستون ساده به نظر میاد، پشتش کلی فیزیک و شیمی خوابیده! وقتی به یه مدل یادگیری ماشین عدد کدورت رو می‌دیم، مدل فقط همون سرنخ رو می‌بینه؛ خودش از روی اون عدد نمی‌فهمه دقیقاً چه ذراتی توی آب شناورن.

خلاصه اینکه آب فقط با نگاه کردن به رنگش قضاوت نمی‌شه و کدورت هم فقط «مقدار گل‌ولای» نیست. توی پست بعدی می‌ریم سراغ سؤال جذاب بعدی: مهندسا چطور این ذرات ریز و لجباز رو به هم می‌چسبونن تا راحت‌تر ته‌نشین و فیلتر بشن؟
